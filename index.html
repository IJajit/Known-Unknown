Finding serendipity between latent space and lived experience

When people work together, ideas surface through small, uneven frictions. Someone makes an offhand joke, somebody else shares an experience, and we bring completely different habits to the same problem and discuss until something clicks. But working with an AI feels different. The model has indexed nearly everything we have ever written down, yet it has never lived through an afternoon.

That gap made me question what actually happens when an AI suggests something clever. Did the system stumble into real serendipity, or am I simply projecting meaning onto statistical output?

Arthur Koestler called creative discovery bisociation in The Act of Creation: the moment two unrelated planes of thought collide to produce a third meaning. In people, that collision draws on personal memory and lived accidents. With machines, it happens geometrically. Looking at Google PAIR’s Embedding Projector, you can see how language models map words into a coordinate system with hundreds of dimensions. Two concepts from different disciplines that seem centuries apart to us might sit right next to each other in that vector space. The model never "thought" of anything; it just traversed a short mathematical distance across latent space.

This also builds on Aby Warburg’s library in Hamburg and his "good neighbor principle." Warburg believed the book you searched for was rarely the one you needed, but the unexpected volume sitting right beside it. Modern search engines usually try to eliminate that wandering, optimizing for predictive answers. But with AI, we rarely know why or how it linked two distant things together. The machine only calculates adjacencies in data; it doesn't know what any of it means. It takes human intuition and lived experience to recognize which collision actually matters, turning that raw connection into an insight.
